## 2. The three challenges

All three share one stated ambition: **simplification for farmers** — cutting complexity in reporting obligations, operational data, and information exchange across the food chain.

**Challenge 1 — "Less time on forms, more time on farms."**
Transform complex, manual, fragmented administrative processes into simplified, automated, integrated reporting systems. Reduce bureaucratic burden, enhance compliance.

**Challenge 2 — "Smarter data, simpler farming."**
Simplify on-field operations by streamlining data collection and analysis, delivering actionable insights and supporting informed decision-making, leveraging precision agriculture technologies such as IoT devices, sensors and connected machinery.

**Challenge 3 — "Flowing data, empowered collaboration."**
Build platforms or tools that simplify information exchange among value chain actors, **empowering farmers to strengthen their market position by achieving fair remuneration, better determination of product value, accessing new sales opportunities**, and building effective partnerships. Farmers stay in control of their data.

**Preference to indicate: Challenge 3 first, Challenge 2 second.** Non-binding, may be revised during the event.

**Why C3.** Its wording is not only about data control — it is explicitly about price discovery, product value and access to new sales channels. That is the closest thing on offer to the surplus-market thinking, and the downstream knowledge (what a processor demands of a supplier, what a lot code and expiry date must carry, where information stops at company boundaries) is the scarce perspective in a room of farm-side participants.

**Why C2 second.** The Commission provides real agricultural datasets, so the lack of own sensor telemetry is not a blocker. "Actionable insights and informed decision-making" is decision support, which is the actual engineering skill here.

**Challenge 1 is a poor fit** and should not be stretched to fit. It is about farm reporting obligations to authorities — CAP declarations, registers, compliance filings. That is not the same as commercial B2B documentation.

## 3. Selection and scoring

A 2+1 level process:

1. **Eligibility check** (mandatory) — citizenship, age, English. Pass/fail.
2. **Evaluation scoring** (mandatory) — scored **1–5 on motivation to participate, area of expertise and core skills**.
3. **Diversity balancing** (preferred, not mandatory) — geographic representation across EU countries and, where possible, gender balance.

For pre-formed teams, expertise and motivation are scored **at team level**, and multidisciplinarity is a positive but non-mandatory criterion.

**Consequence: the free-text fields are the entire application.** Only *area of expertise* and *motivation statement* are scored. Everything else is a checkbox. The form collects name, email, nationality, country of residence, area of expertise, motivation statement, challenge preference.

**Realistic odds:** likely selected — not because the profile is impressive by sector standards, but because a first-edition online event needs builders, and agri hackathons are always short on people who can actually ship. Romania is also underrepresented relative to Western Europe, which helps at step 3.

## 4. Deliverables

Each team must submit:
- **A pitch deck** — the problem, the solution, its feasibility and potential impact
- **A prototype, wireframe, code, or functional demo** — note that a **wireframe is acceptable**; code is not mandatory

Final submissions are followed by a pitch to the jury.

**Open source is explicitly rewarded** — "strongly encouraged... viewed positively during evaluation."

**Original work rule:** *"submit only original work created during the hackathon period **and declare any pre-existing code or tools used**."* Reuse is therefore permitted **if declared**. Nothing needs to be hidden and nothing needs to start from zero.

## 6. Strategy

### Goals — three things that are controllable

1. **3–5 named contacts from different countries**, emailed within a week of the event.
2. **Answers to five research questions**, from sources that are not Vladislav.
3. A decent submission — which follows from the first two.

Winning is not the goal and is largely outside anyone's control (team assignment, jury taste).

### The five research questions

Ask on day one, not day three:

- What happens today to stock approaching its date? Who buys it, at what discount, through whom?
- Do you have visibility of your own stock across locations, or is it separate spreadsheets?
- Does anyone you know use demand forecasting software? What do they pay?
- Do you sell to HoReCa or processors? How do you find them?
- Is the 2030 food-waste reduction target discussed concretely where you are, or is it still paper?

The last one matters most: it establishes whether Directive (EU) 2025/1892 is a real tailwind or only a good paragraph in a funding application.

### What not to do

- **Do not build the company's product there.** IP is retained by the creating *team* — which means shared with three people met days earlier. Bring the thinking and small components, rewritten; not the codebase.
- **Do not claim agri-food expertise.** There is none. What exists is downstream FMCG knowledge, acquired secondhand, plus a month of regulatory reading.
- **Do not call Frigo a product.** It is a food management app built for a hackathon, with no users. Calling it a product invites a question with no good answer.
- **Do not imply a company exists.** No entity has been registered.

### Honest limits

First edition, so no track record. Fully online, so relationships are thinner than in-person. Every challenge is farmer-centric while the intended customer is the FMCG processor — adjacent, not central.

### Motivation (1980 chars)

I came to agriculture from the other end of the chain. I built a food management application, and through it met an operator with two decades in FMCG distribution in Serbia and Bosnia, who described how much stock expires simply because nobody sells it in time.

The problem itself is not in question: EU figures place 18% of food waste at processing and manufacturing against 7% at retail and distribution, and Directive (EU) 2025/1892 now sets a binding 10% reduction target for that stage by 2030. What is still open is the mechanism. My reading is that much of it is an information failure: the people who would have bought that stock never knew it existed while it was still sellable, and the data that would have told them sits fragmented across companies with no incentive to share it.

I want to build against that, not survey it. In a team with a farmer, an economist and someone from policy, I am the one who turns an idea into something that runs before the pitch. I also bring a view from downstream: secondhand, from that one operator, but enough to arrive with specific questions rather than general ones.

Why this problem and not another: the most rigorous thing I have built was a research system that answered its question properly and helped nobody. I work differently when there is someone on the other end of the output. Here there is one. A producer who cannot sell in time loses money, and food that was grown is thrown away.

It matters that it is this event. The directive I keep citing is the Commission's own, and here the people who wrote it and the people it lands on are in the same room, with real datasets. Everything I know stops at the farm gate; this is the one room where the other side is the default. It is also why I want to leave with a few people in other countries I can keep asking long after the weekend.

I am not coming to find out whether the problem is real. I am coming to help build against it, which is what Challenge 3 asks for.

### Why this challenge (1800 chars)

Challenge 3 first, Challenge 2 second.

Challenge 3 names three things in one breath, fair remuneration, better determination of product value, and access to new sales opportunities, and then adds that farmers should stay in control of their data. I read those as one problem rather than four. Each depends on a record the farmer generates and does not keep: what was harvested, in which lot, at what quality, how long it will hold, and what happened to it after it left the gate.

That record is what I would want to work on. Today it is retyped, degraded or dropped at each handover, then reconstructed badly by everyone further down. Whoever holds it when the price is agreed holds the position, and it is never the person who produced the goods. A farmer with a durable, portable record of their own product argues from evidence rather than from the buyer's version of events. The same record is what makes it possible to find a buyer for a lot before it becomes unsellable. One missing artefact, two consequences: the price that cannot be argued, and the food that does not move in time.

What I bring to that is specific rather than general. I have gone through one market in detail, which fields actually survive a handover between companies and which get retyped or lost, and checked the regulatory half of it against the source texts rather than summaries. How much of that generalises I do not know. A team with farmers in it would tell me in the first hour.

Challenge 2 second, because the Commission is providing real datasets and turning messy operational data into decision support is the work I do. Not Challenge 1: reporting obligations towards authorities are a real burden, but they are a different problem from commercial exchange between companies, and I would be guessing at it.
